## 1 (a) | Describe | 6

*Candidates are asked to describe Mori and Arai's (2010) study, "No Need to Fake It".*

Responses may include:

- the aim was to replicate Asch's experiment without actors, so that nobody was acting unnaturally and to increase ecological validity **(1)**
- it was a lab experiment testing normative influence with judgement of lines **(1)**
- everyone wore filter glasses, like those for a 3D movie, letting them look at the same image but see different things **(1)**
- this is the MORI technique (Manipulation of Overlapping Rivalrous Images by polarizing filters) **(1)**
- everyone was a true participant, but one had a different filter and so perceived a different correct answer **(1)**
- participants were told the glasses protected their eyes from the glare **(1)**
- there were 104 Japanese Psychology students (40 male and 64 female) in groups of four **(1)**
- answers were stated aloud, with the minority participant going third **(1)**
- females conformed on 4.41 out of 12 critical trials, similar to Asch's findings **(1)**, and males did not conform **(1)**
- having an "ally" made very little difference **(1)**

## 1 (b) | Evaluate | 4

*Candidates are asked to evaluate Mori and Arai's study into conformity.*

Responses may include strengths alone, weaknesses alone, or both.

Where candidates provide only generic evaluations, **a maximum of 2 marks** can be awarded.

Responses may include:

- it used both genders in a good sample **(1)**, so results can be generalised to both males and females **(1)**
- there were no confederates, all were true participants **(1)**, so there wasn't any acting **(1)**
- participants knew each other **(1)**, which is more relevant to real-world conformity than conforming to strangers **(1)**
- it was a tightly controlled lab experiment **(1)**, able to control extraneous variables **(1)**
- the technique could be used in further research, for example with children **(1)**, where confederates would not be practical **(1)**
- it still used deception **(1)**, which raises ethical concerns **(1)**
- it is still an artificial, unusual situation **(1)**, despite higher ecological validity than Asch's **(1)**
- it took place in Japan, a collectivist culture **(1)**, so cannot be generalised to other cultures **(1)**
- participants might have known of Asch's experiment **(1)**, questioning the reliability of results **(1)**
- only students were used **(1)**, so a small age range was targeted **(1)**

## 1 (c) | Analyse | 12

*Candidates are asked to analyse Mori and Arai's (2010) investigation into conformity.*

Award **a maximum of 3 marks** for descriptions of the study.

Award **a maximum of 3 marks** for evaluation of the study.

Candidates can gain the full **12 marks** for analysis.

Points of analysis may include:

- the study supports normative social influence: participants were sure of the answer as the task was easy, so they went along with it to fit in **(1)**
- women conforming more links to Eagly and Carli: females may be more concerned with group harmony and cohesion **(1)**
- the researchers explained the gender difference through the different expectations and social roles of males and females **(1)**
- participants knew each other, unlike in Asch's study, suggesting greater external validity, as real-life conformity tends to occur among acquainted people **(1)**
- even when we are not unsure of the right answer, the group has a considerable power to change our behaviour **(1)**
- both this study and Asch's found conformity, which implies conformity still occurs in 2010 **(1)**
- Asch found males conformed on a third of critical trials, and here males did not conform at all **(1)**, which may reflect generational changes in the 55 years since Asch **(1)**, or culture, as this was 2010s Japan and Asch was 1950s America **(1)**
- having no confederates makes this study truer to real-life situations than Asch's, making the results more ecologically valid **(1)**
- females conformed on 4.41/12 trials, whereas Perrin and Spencer, using engineering, mathematics and chemistry students, found conformity on only one out of 396 trials **(1)**
- the findings show how people might join in with bullying to avoid being disliked by the group **(1)**, follow trends **(1)**, or be susceptible to peer pressure, for example smoking **(1)**
