## 1 (a) | Describe | 5

*Candidates are asked to describe Milgram's study of obedience.*

Responses may include:

- Milgram examined justifications at the Nuremberg trials, where the defence was often that the accused were just following orders **(1)**
- the aim was to find out if ordinary people would obey an authority figure when asked to inflict pain **(1)**
- it was a lab experiment at Yale University **(1)**
- 40 volunteers, American men aged 20-50, were recruited for a "memory and learning experiment" and paid $4.50 **(1)**
- an experimenter in a white lab coat introduced each participant to "Mr Wallace", actually a confederate **(1)**
- fixed lots meant Mr Wallace was always the learner and the true participant always the teacher **(1)**
- the teacher gave a shock for each wrong answer, increasing the level on a generator from 15 to 450 volts **(1)**
- the participant received a mild 15-volt shock as an example, and switches were labelled, for example 375V "Danger! Severe shock" **(1)**
- the confederate deliberately gave wrong answers, shouted in pain, mentioned a heart condition, and was silent after 315V **(1)**
- hesitant participants received verbal prods, from "Please continue" to "You have no other choice but to continue" **(1)**
- 26 out of 40 (65%) continued to 450 volts **(1)**, and nobody stopped before 300 volts **(1)**
- 14 broke off earlier, and there was a high incidence of sweating, trembling and stuttering **(1)**

## 1 (b) | Explain | 4

*Candidates are asked to explain what conclusions can be reached from Milgram's study.*

Responses may include:

- in stressful situations when responsibility can be passed to another, people are likely to follow orders from a person with perceived authority **(1)**
- ordinary people are extremely obedient to authority, even when asked to behave in an inhumane manner **(1)**
- it is not necessarily evil people who commit atrocities, but ordinary people obeying authority **(1)**
- people are in an agentic state when they follow orders given by an authority figure **(1)**

## 1 (c) | Evaluate | 8

*Candidates are asked to evaluate Milgram's (1963) study of obedience.*

Where candidates provide only generic evaluations, **a maximum of half marks** can be awarded.

Responses may include:

- it was influential and well controlled in a lab, including the prods and structure **(1)**, showing that it is a reliable method **(1)**
- pre-scripted prods and tape-recorded responses gave a standardised procedure **(1)**, so it can be carried out repeatedly to achieve similar results **(1)**
- it links to real-life examples like soldiers in Nazi Germany **(1)**, showing the results are highly applicable **(1)**
- it was unethical, as participants thought it was a study about memory **(1)**. They were deceived and may have acted abnormally, so results are unreliable **(1)**
- it was difficult to withdraw, as the experimenter prompted participants to continue **(1)**
- some participants felt extremely stressed and guilty afterwards **(1)**, so were not protected from harm **(1)**. However, in a follow-up survey 84% were glad to have been involved, and showed no signs of long-term harm **(1)**
- it lacked ecological validity, as teachers are not asked to shock learners in real life **(1)**, so participants may act differently from in a natural setting **(1)**
- the sample was 40 men **(1)**, so results cannot be generalised to females or other populations **(1)**
- critics claim participants were play-acting **(1)**, which suggests demand characteristics and lowers validity **(1)**
