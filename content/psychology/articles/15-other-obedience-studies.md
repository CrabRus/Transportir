## 1 (a) | Describe | 6

*Candidates are asked to describe other studies of obedience.*

Responses may include:

- Hofling: a doctor over the phone instructed nurses to give an unknown medication, astroten **(1)**. The nurses did not know they were in an experiment, and 21 out of 22 (95%) obeyed **(1)**
- Rank and Jacobson (1977): only 2 out of 18 nurses obeyed orders to give a lethal dose of valium **(1)**, because they had knowledge of what valium was **(1)**
- Burger (2009) replicated Milgram with not as many volts and fewer prods **(1)**, and found obedience only slightly lower than Milgram's **(1)**
- Bickman (1974): people obeyed a security guard more than a person with no uniform or in a milkman uniform **(1)**

## 1 (b) | Analyse | 8

*Candidates are asked to compare and contrast Milgram's study with other studies of obedience.*

It is not enough to state only that studies are similar or different. Candidates should explain why it matters.

Points of analysis may include:

**Bickman (1974)**

- both found that uniform played a part in obedience, showing that it is a key determinant of why people obey **(1)**
- Milgram used a laboratory and Bickman the field, so Milgram has greater reliability and Bickman greater ecological validity **(1)**

**Hofling (1966)**

- both support perceived legitimate authority, as people are very unwilling to question supposed "authority" **(1)**
- it took place in a real hospital environment, so has more ecological validity, meaning Milgram's results can be questioned **(1)**
- nurses obeyed on 95% of occasions compared to Milgram's 65% **(1)**
- orders came by phone, so it goes against proximity to authority **(1)**

**Rank and Jacobson (1977)**

- both support proximity to authority **(1)**
- nurses knew the drug and few obeyed, unlike with Hofling's unknown drug **(1)**

**Burger (2009)**

- obedience rates were similar, suggesting Milgram's findings remain relevant in modern contexts **(1)**
- it was carried out under more ethical circumstances **(1)**

**Zimbardo**

- both link to perceived legitimate authority, but Zimbardo's is more like a real-life situation **(1)**
