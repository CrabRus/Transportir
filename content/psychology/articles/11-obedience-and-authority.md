## 1 (a) | Describe | 4

*Candidates are asked to describe obedience and how it differs from conformity.*

Responses may include:

- obedience is a form of compliance that occurs when people follow direct commands, usually from someone in authority **(1)**
- it does not necessarily mean that private opinions have been changed **(1)**
- it is a response to a direct order from a figure with perceived legitimate authority **(1)**, and the individual responds in a way they would not otherwise have done **(1)**
- conformity is yielding to group pressure, and nobody actually has to ask **(1)**, whereas in obedience authority figures such as parents, teachers and police give direct instructions **(1)**
- we conform to be accepted or to fit in **(1)**, whereas we obey to avoid punishment or unpleasant consequences **(1)**

## 1 (b) | Explain | 6

*Candidates are asked to explain obedience and perceived legitimate authority using research evidence.*

Where candidates only provide descriptions, **a maximum of 3 marks** can be awarded.

Responses may include:

- societies are structured as hierarchies, and figures such as a boss or head teacher are perceived legitimate authority figures **(1)**
- sometimes legitimacy is unclear, leading us to ask "does this person have the right to tell me what to do?", as with babysitters or student teachers **(1)**
- if legitimate authority is in question, obedience levels tend to drop **(1)**
- we look for clues about legitimate authority, such as clothing and demands **(1)**
- in Bickman (1974), three people told passers-by to do things like "pick up that litter" **(1)**
- obedience was 89% for a security guard, 57% for a milkman and 33% for a civilian **(1)**
- the guard had no right to tell the public what to do, but the uniform conveyed a sense of power **(1)**
- obedience is likely when the order comes from a perceived authority, when there is a perceived punishment, or when we are unsure and pass the responsibility **(1)**

## 1 (c) | Describe | 3

*Candidates are asked to describe Max Weber's sources of legitimate authority.*

Candidates must refer to all three sources for full marks.

- tradition: some groups, such as parents and teachers, are considered to have the right to give instructions **(1)**
- legal: we obey because not doing so could lead to punishment **(1)**
- charisma: some people, such as politicians, have great personal charm and persuade others **(1)**
